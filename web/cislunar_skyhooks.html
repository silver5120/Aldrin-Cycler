Earth–Moon momentum-exchange tethers · study, Nov 2026

Cislunar Skyhooks

A spinning cable in orbit can catch a payload at its slow-moving bottom tip and fling it from its fast-moving top tip. No propellant leaves the system; the tether pays in orbital momentum. The question is where the physics and the risk let you build one first.

Start at the MoonA lunar tip needs about 1.6 km/s. Today's fibers can do that at 2–5× the payload mass.
Earth side: start in LEOCatch payloads that are already in orbit. Don't try to catch from the atmosphere.
Keep each tether under ~2 km/sAbove that, tether mass grows exponentially. Split big jobs across tethers or let the vehicle do part of the job.

The physics wall

Why tip speed decides everything

A spinning tether has to hold itself together as well as the payload. Each fiber has a characteristic velocity, Vc = √(2σ / SF·ρ). Required tether mass grows roughly as e(V/Vc)² (Moravec; Hoyt). For today's best fibers, Vc is about 1.5–2 km/s. A lunar tip is under that limit. An Earth skyhook that catches from the atmosphere is far beyond it.

The y-axis is tether mass ÷ payload mass on a log scale. These are ideal tapered tethers. Redundant multi-strand construction, the grapple, and the central station add about 1.5–3× more. The ballast mass needed to absorb momentum is separate.

Risk

How dangerous is each side?

The Earth side is far more dangerous. The physics is harder there, and the orbits it shares are crowded with things that belong to other people.

HazardEarth side (LEO)Moon side (low lunar orbit)
Debris & micrometeoroid cutsHighIn 1994, SEDS-2's single-strand tether was cut after about 4 days. A tether hundreds of km long is a big target, so it must be multi-strand (a "Hoytether"), where one cut doesn't sever it.LowMicrometeoroids only. There is no debris population from past launches. Earth side

Start in the atmosphere or in LEO?

Start in LEO. Rockets already deliver payloads to LEO cheaply, so the tether only has to add the next 1.5–2 km/s. Catching from the atmosphere means building a hypersonic aircraft that doesn't exist and a tether whose mass is out of reach.

Not before 2060

Atmospheric catch (Boeing HASTOL)

A Mach 10–17 aircraft at ~100 km hands its payload to a tip moving 3.5–4 km/s. The tether would weigh 100–3,000× the payload. The fastest air-breather so far, X-43A, sustained Mach 9.6 for about 10 seconds. The rendezvous window lasts seconds, with heating at the tip.

2050s+, if fibers improve

Suborbital rocket catch

A reusable booster lofts the payload to ~150–200 km at ~4–5 km/s, and the tip covers the remaining ~3 km/s. That still takes 30–300× the payload in tether mass. It's attractive only if carbon-nanotube or graphene fibers reach the market.

Earliest Earth-side option

Orbit-to-orbit from LEO (MXER / Hoyt)

The payload rides a normal rocket to LEO. A tether in an elliptical orbit (perigee 400 km, apogee ~11,000 km) catches it at 7.67 km/s with its bottom tip and throws it at 10.76 km/s from its top tip. That's the full 3.1 km/s from a tip spinning only ~1.5 km/s. Tether mass is 2.5–5× the payload. Between throws, an electrodynamic tether pushes on Earth's magnetic field to restore momentum without propellant.

The catch nobody puts on the poster

Momentum is the real budget

Lifting 1 t from the Moon's surface and throwing it at 3.2 km/s takes 3,200 t·m/s of momentum out of the tether. If the station has 50× the payload's mass, its speed drops about 64 m/s. In the circular 150 km orbit shown above, that would lower the far side of the orbit by roughly 300 km, straight into the ground. There are three fixes, and real designs use all of them:

  • Elliptical orbit, operations at periapsis. A momentum loss at periapsis mostly lowers the apoapsis, so the catch point stays put. This is why Hoyt's "Moon-Whip" design uses an eccentric orbit.
  • Balance traffic. Catching Earth cargo and setting it down adds momentum, and lifting lunar exports removes it. An early lunar base imports more than it exports, which leaves a surplus the tether can spend on lifts.
  • Make up the difference with electric thrusters. Restoring 2 km/s·t per lift with 3,000 s Isp burns about 70 kg of propellant per tonne lifted. A chemical lander flying the same trip burns about 700 kg per tonne, plus the lander's own mass.
Recommendation

A staged architecture

Each phase removes the biggest unknown before the next one spends money. No rotating tether has ever caught a payload in orbit, so that comes first.

Phase 0 · 2027–2031 · tens of $M

Catch demo in LEO

  • Two smallsats spin a 1–10 km multi-strand tether. The tip autonomously grapples and releases a cooperative target at tens of m/s.
  • Fly a second tether to show electrodynamic reboost. Use short-lived orbits below 400 km so failures reenter quickly.
  • Retires the catch mechanism, spin control, and strand-cut survival risks.
Phase 1 · early–mid 2030s

Lunar partial rotovator, cargo only

  • Near-polar frozen orbit serving the south-pole ice sites. 80 km arm, ~1.0 km/s tip, tip passing about 30 km above the surface.
  • Hoppers fly a ~0.7 km/s suborbital hop to meet the tip, instead of the ~1.9 km/s it takes to reach orbit and back. They never fly near the terrain at speed.
  • Tether mass about 1–2× the payload. Ballast comes from spent landers, later from bagged regolith. Solar-electric thrusters provide reboost.
Phase 2 · late 2030s

Full lunar rotovator, crew-rated

  • 150 km arm, ~1.6 km/s tip touching down at near-zero speed, 1.8 g at the tip. Landing and launch need almost no propellant, apart from precision trims.
  • A full rotovator throws payloads at ~3.2 km/s, which is beyond lunar escape. It can send oxygen and water straight onto Earth-return trajectories.
Phase 3 · 2040s

LEO booster tether, balanced by lunar oxygen

  • Elliptical-orbit tether (perigee 400 km, apogee ~11,000 km, ~1.5 km/s tip) with electrodynamic reboost, supplying the whole 3.1 km/s to the Moon. Rocket ΔV for LEO ↔ lunar surface drops from 5.8 km/s to ~0.2 km/s each way. Its perigee operations are coordinated around the megaconstellation shells.
  • Lunar oxygen sent down to LEO depot customers restores momentum. This is the closed cislunar loop Hoyt and Forward proposed.
Phase 4 · only if fibers improve

Suborbital-catch Earth skyhook

  • Worth revisiting only if nanotube or graphene fiber with a characteristic velocity above ~3 km/s becomes a commercial product. The atmospheric-catch HASTOL design stays shelved.
What has flown

Heritage and the gap

Tethers tens of km long have been deployed several times. Spinning a tether and catching a payload with it has never been done.

MissionYearWhat happened
SEDS-11993Deployed a 20 km hanging tether and released the end mass into reentry. This was the first momentum-exchange release.
SEDS-21994Held a 20 km tether stable, but the single strand was cut after about 4 days.
TSS-1R (Shuttle)1996Deployed 19.7 km before the tether broke from electrical arcing. It produced electrodynamic current.
TiPS1996A 4 km tether survived in orbit for about a decade.
YES22007Deployed 31.7 km, the longest structure ever placed in space.
KITE (HTV-6)2017An electrodynamic tether that failed to deploy.
